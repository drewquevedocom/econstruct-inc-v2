Accessibility compliance touches nearly every part of a commercial project — the parking lot, the entrance, the path of travel, the restrooms, and the counters and controls inside the space — and California enforces a stricter standard than the federal ADA baseline through Title 24 of the state building code.

## Title 24 Versus Federal ADA

The federal ADA sets a nationwide minimum, but California's Title 24 accessibility standards are frequently more restrictive, particularly around restroom clearances, door hardware, and accessible route slope. Plan check in California is measured against Title 24, so designing only to the federal minimum will not pass local review.

## The Path of Travel Requirement

A common surprise for tenants and building owners is that a tenant improvement project, even a modest one, can trigger a requirement to bring the accessible path of travel to the space up to current code — including entrances, parking, and restrooms that serve it, not just the leased space itself. This scope needs to be identified during preconstruction, not discovered during plan check.

## Where Compliance Most Often Fails in the Field

Restroom grab bar placement, clear floor space at fixtures, door clear widths, and accessible route cross-slope are among the most common field failures, usually because small framing or slab tolerances shift a dimension just enough to fail inspection. These items are far cheaper to verify during rough framing than to correct after finishes are installed.

## Building Compliance in From Design

The most reliable way to avoid accessibility corrections is to verify Title 24 dimensions against approved drawings at each construction milestone — rough framing, wall board, and finish — rather than relying on the drawings alone to guarantee field accuracy. This is a construction management discipline, not just a design responsibility.
