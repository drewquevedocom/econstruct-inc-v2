Food manufacturing facilities that include any cooking process, blanching step, or fryer line need a Type 1 exhaust hood system, and that system is one of the most heavily scrutinized elements of the entire building permit. Getting the hood, exhaust, make-up air, and fire suppression design right before submittal avoids weeks of plan check corrections that stall the whole permit, not just the kitchen scope.

## What Triggers a Type 1 Hood Requirement

Any equipment producing grease-laden vapor requires a Type 1 hood: fryers, griddles, char-broilers, and most combination ovens used in food production. The threshold is the cooking process, not the size of the facility, so a manufacturing line with production-scale fryers needs the same category of hood as a restaurant kitchen, scaled to a much higher exhaust volume.

## Exhaust and Make-Up Air Design

Hood exhaust volume drives make-up air requirements, and undersized make-up air is one of the most common field problems on manufacturing kitchens: without enough replacement air, the hood cannot maintain capture velocity and the space depressurizes, pulling conditioned air from adjacent production areas. This has to be sized by a mechanical engineer against the specific equipment schedule, not estimated from a generic hood size chart.

## Fire Suppression Integration

NFPA 96 requires a wet chemical fire suppression system integrated with the hood, ducting, and cooking equipment, tied to an automatic fuel and electrical shutoff. The suppression contractor, hood fabricator, and mechanical contractor all touch this system, and their work has to be sequenced and inspected together — a hood that passes mechanical inspection but has not been coordinated with suppression nozzle placement will fail fire department sign-off.

## Duct Clearances and Structural Coordination

Grease duct requires fire-rated enclosure or minimum clearance to combustibles, and the duct routing has to be resolved with structural and other trades early, since a duct run added after roof framing is set becomes a significant rework item. This is a preconstruction coordination problem as much as a mechanical design problem.

## Plan Check and Fire Department Review

In most Los Angeles County jurisdictions, the hood and suppression system goes through both building plan check and a separate fire department review, and the two reviews do not always run on the same timeline. Submitting complete, coordinated hood and suppression drawings up front, rather than in separate uncoordinated packages, is the single biggest lever for avoiding duplicate correction cycles.
