Office tenant improvements involve at least three parties with different priorities: the building owner protecting the base building and the lease, the tenant wanting a space that fits their operations on schedule and on budget, and the contractor managing both. Most TI disputes trace back to expectations that were never explicitly aligned at the start.

## What the Tenant Improvement Allowance Actually Covers

The TI allowance in a lease is a per-square-foot dollar figure the landlord contributes toward build-out, and it rarely covers everything a tenant wants. Design has to be built around that number from the earliest test-fit, since discovering a budget gap after drawings are complete forces a costly redesign.

## Test-Fit: Aligning Space Plan to Budget and Operations

Test-fit is where the tenant's space program gets checked against the available square footage, the TI allowance, and any building-standard constraints from the owner. This step should surface conflicts between what the tenant wants and what the budget or building systems can support, before permit drawings are started.

## What the Building Owner Reviews and Controls

Owners typically review TI drawings for compliance with base building systems, lease restrictions, and any building-standard finish requirements, and may require their own engineer to review structural or mechanical modifications. Tenants who route drawings through owner review early avoid late-stage conflicts that can stall permit submittal.

## Setting a Realistic Construction Timeline

A realistic office TI schedule accounts for test-fit, permitting, and construction as three distinct phases, each with its own risk of delay — permitting in particular can vary widely across Los Angeles jurisdictions. Building the schedule around actual jurisdiction timelines, not a generic estimate, is what keeps a TI project on track.
