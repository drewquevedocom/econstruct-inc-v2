A retail build-out has to satisfy the landlord, the city, and the opening date all at once, and none of the three will wait for the others. Getting this right means treating landlord design criteria and city permitting as parallel tracks that need coordinated drawings, not sequential steps that can be handled one after another.

## Landlord Design Criteria Comes First

Most shopping centers and mixed-use buildings issue a design criteria manual covering storefront materials, signage size and placement, and allowable construction methods. This document has to be reviewed before drawings are finalized, since landlord standards are frequently stricter than city code minimums and will not be waived by a city-issued permit.

## Landlord Approval Is a Separate Gate From the City Permit

Many centers require the landlord's property management or design review team to approve construction drawings before the tenant can even submit for a city permit, and some require a security deposit or construction insurance certificate as a condition of approval. Skipping or rushing this step is one of the most common causes of a stalled retail build-out.

## City Permitting for Retail Tenant Space

Once landlord approval is secured, the drawings go through standard tenant improvement plan check with the city, covering means of egress, accessibility, and life safety systems specific to the retail use. Running this in parallel with landlord review, rather than after it, is the difference between a build-out that opens on schedule and one that does not.

## Protecting the Opening Date

Retail schedules are almost always anchored to a fixed opening date tied to a lease, marketing plan, or seasonal window. Protecting that date means identifying the longest-lead item early, whether it is landlord approval, long-lead storefront glazing, or a specific piece of equipment, and building the schedule backward from opening rather than forward from lease signing.
