## Navigating Permits Before Construction Begins

One of the most common mistakes first-time restaurant owners make is underestimating the time and complexity of the Los Angeles permitting process. Before a single wall is framed, your project must secure a building permit from [LADBS](https://www.ladbs.org/permits) and a food facility permit from the [LA County Department of Public Health](https://ehservices.publichealth.lacounty.gov/). Both agencies review full plan sets — architectural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and structural drawings — and their review timelines run in parallel, typically eight weeks for LADBS and four weeks for the Health Department. Projects that submit incomplete drawings or that miss health department requirements in the architectural plans face correction cycles that extend the permitting phase by months.

A restaurant contractor with deep Los Angeles experience knows exactly how each agency reviewer reads a set of plans. They will specify the correct equipment clearances, hand-washing sink locations, ventilation rates, and grease trap sizing in the original drawings so that corrections are minimized. Understanding the [ideal restaurant construction timeline](/blog/what-is-the-ideal-restaurant-construction-timeline) from design through permit approval through construction close-out is essential for setting realistic expectations with investors, landlords, and your own operations team.

## Restaurant Construction Budgeting and Cost Management

Launching a restaurant in Los Angeles requires careful financial planning across several cost categories that are easy to underestimate. Build-out costs typically run $150 to $400-plus per square foot depending on finish level, kitchen complexity, and MEP scope. Equipment packages for a full-service kitchen can add $100,000 to $300,000 on top of construction. Permitting fees for a tenant improvement project — which cover building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and health permits — commonly total $10,000 to $25,000 depending on project valuation. Budget a 15% to 25% contingency on top of your base construction estimate to absorb unforeseen site conditions, utility upgrades required by the landlord, or changes triggered by plan check corrections.

Value engineering is a critical discipline that a skilled design-build contractor applies throughout the design phase. By selecting materials, mechanical systems, and kitchen layout configurations that achieve your design intent at lower cost, an experienced contractor can often recover 10% to 15% of project cost relative to a first-draft design. This is a direct benefit of the design-build model, where the team producing the drawings is simultaneously responsible for building within the agreed budget.
