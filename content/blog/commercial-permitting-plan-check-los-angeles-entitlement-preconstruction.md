Commercial permitting in Los Angeles is not one process — it is two, and treating them as one is where most schedule surprises come from. Entitlement confirms what a site is legally allowed to be used for. Plan check confirms that a specific set of drawings meets code. A project can pass one and still fail the other, and the order in which a team addresses them determines whether preconstruction takes months or a year.

## Entitlement: Confirming the Use Before Designing Around It

Zoning, conditional use permits, and any discretionary approval have to be confirmed before significant design spend, not after. A restaurant use in a space zoned for retail, or a change of occupancy that triggers a conditional use permit, can add months to a project timeline if it is discovered after the design is already drawn. This is a preconstruction question, and it should be answered in the first weeks of a project, not during permit submittal.

## Plan Check: Where Design Meets Code

Once entitlement is confirmed, plan check reviews the construction drawings against the building, fire, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing codes. In Los Angeles, plan check timelines vary significantly by jurisdiction and project complexity, and multiple agencies may review the same project on different timelines — building, fire, health department, and sometimes a specific overlay zone review.

## Building a Realistic Preconstruction Schedule Around Permitting

The teams that avoid permitting surprises build their preconstruction schedule around the permitting strategy, not the other way around. That means confirming entitlement early, submitting complete and coordinated drawing packages rather than incremental ones, and identifying which agencies will review the project before the first submittal — not discovering a required health department or fire department review midway through plan check.

## Common Entitlement and Plan Check Pitfalls

The most frequent pitfalls are designing before confirming zoning and use, submitting plan check packages with uncoordinated mechanical, fire, and structural drawings, and underestimating how many separate agencies a single commercial project can touch. Each of these is avoidable with a disciplined preconstruction process, and each is expensive to fix once discovered mid-review.
